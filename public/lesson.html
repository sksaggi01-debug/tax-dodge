Assumes the scarce units go to the buyers who value them most. Real rationing (lines, luck, black markets) usually makes buyers worse off than shown.

Worked example · Rent control

Price Ceiling at $4: Step by Step

Step 1 · Quantities at $4
Qd = 120 − 10(4) = 80    Qs = 20(4) − 30 = 50
Shortage = 80 − 50 = 30 units

Step 2 · Only 50 units trade
Buyers' value of the 50th unit: P = (120 − 50)/10 = $7

Step 3 · Surplus
CS = ½ × [(12 − 4) + (7 − 4)] × 50 = $275.00
PS = ½ × (4 − 1.50) × 50 = $62.50
DWL = ½ × (7 − 4) × (70 − 50) = $30.00
Check: 275 + 62.50 + 30 = 367.50 ✓

Step 4 · If a black market forms
Illegal resale up to $7: (7 − 4) × 50 = $150 moves from buyers to black-market sellers → CS falls to $125

What changed vs. equilibrium?

  • Consumers who get an apartment gain (CS $245 → $275).
  • Landlords lose (PS $122.50 → $62.50).
  • $30 of trades that would help both sides never happen = DWL.
  • 30 renters want a unit at $4 and cannot find one.
Check: the ceiling is lowered to $3. What is the shortage?

A floor only binds when it is set above equilibrium ($5).

Qs at floor

Consumer surplus
Producer surplus
Deadweight loss
Illegal discounts to buyers

Assumes the government does not buy the unsold units, and the lowest-cost sellers make the sales. Black market: sellers with unsold output secretly undercut the floor (e.g., paying workers cash below minimum wage), down to their cost of the last unit sold.

Worked example · Minimum wage logic

Price Floor at $6: Step by Step

Step 1 · Quantities at $6
Qd = 120 − 10(6) = 60    Qs = 20(6) − 30 = 90
Surplus = 90 − 60 = 30 units unsold

Step 2 · Only 60 units trade
Sellers' cost of the 60th unit: P = (60 + 30)/20 = $4.50

Step 3 · Surplus
CS = ½ × (12 − 6) × 60 = $180.00
PS = ½ × [(6 − 1.50) + (6 − 4.50)] × 60 = $180.00
DWL = ½ × (6 − 4.50) × (70 − 60) = $7.50
Check: 180 + 180 + 7.50 = 367.50 ✓

Step 4 · If a black market forms
Illegal sales down to $4.50: (6 − 4.50) × 60 = $90 moves from sellers to buyers → PS falls to $90

Apply it: minimum wage

  • The "price" is the wage; sellers are workers, buyers are employers.
  • Workers who keep jobs gain (PS $122.50 → $180).
  • The unsold surplus = people who want work at that wage but are not hired.
  • Size of the effect depends on how far above equilibrium the floor is.
Check: the floor is raised to $7. How many units are actually sold?

Worked example · $3 per-unit tax

Tax Incidence: Step by Step

Step 1 · New equilibrium (tax on sellers)
Sellers keep Pb − 3:  120 − 10Pb = 20(Pb − 3) − 30
180 = 30Pb → Pb = $7,  Ps = $4,  Q = 50

Step 2 · Split the $3
Buyers: 7 − 5 = $2 (⅔)  ·  Sellers: 5 − 4 = $1 (⅓)
Formula: Es/(Es+|Ed|) = 1.43/(1.43+0.71) = ⅔ ✓

Step 3 · Areas
Revenue = 3 × 50 = $150 (buyers $100, sellers $50)
CS = ½ × (12 − 7) × 50 = $125.00
PS = ½ × (4 − 1.50) × 50 = $62.50
DWL = ½ × 3 × (70 − 50) = $30.00
Check: 125 + 62.50 + 150 + 30 = 367.50 ✓

Key idea

  • Who writes the check to the government does not decide who pays.
  • Here supply is more elastic (1.43 vs 0.71), so buyers carry ⅔.
  • DWL: 20 trades worth more than their cost are lost.
Check: the tax is cut to $1.50. What price do buyers pay?

Three rules to remember

  • Binding ceiling (below P*) → shortage; binding floor (above P*) → surplus.
  • Every binding control or tax reduces quantity and creates DWL.
  • The less elastic side bears more of a tax.
